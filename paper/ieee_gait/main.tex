\begin{abstract}
Gait verification is appealing because a wearable can check identity without interrupting its owner. Its usefulness, however, depends on what happens after enrollment: the person may become fatigued, return days later, or walk while doing something else. We study these changes using sacrum accelerometer and gyroscope data from 16 DUO-GAIT participants. A statistical-feature support vector machine (SVM), trained and calibrated on ordinary single-task walking, provides a leakage-aware problem assessment. Its mean false rejection/false acceptance rates are 1.49/1.49\% on held-out normal walking, 19.13/11.26\% after same-visit fatigue, 23.04/10.15\% on later dual-task walking, and 54.33/13.95\% on later fatigued dual-task walking. We then develop a population-trained Siamese encoder that learns condition variation from normal and fatigued walks of development participants while requiring only normal walking from each new user. Using acceleration and angular-velocity magnitude time series, its 2-second decisions obtain FRR/FAR of 20.95/14.53\% on DT-control and 45.92/15.30\% on DT-fatigue. Averaging consecutive scores over 11 seconds changes these rates to 16.33/11.41\% and 47.55/13.06\%, respectively. The results show that population-level condition learning and temporal evidence can improve normal-only gait enrollment, with the strongest balanced gain on later non-fatigued dual-task walking.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Continuous authentication provides ongoing verification of a user's identity after initial device access. Unlike conventional authentication, which verifies identity only at login, continuous authentication uses behavioral or physiological signals collected during normal device use to detect possible changes in ownership. Behavioral signals are attractive for this purpose because they can be observed during normal use. Gait is one such signal: accelerometers and gyroscopes already present in mobile and wearable devices can register repeated body motion while the owner walks. Early accelerometer studies established the feasibility of recognizing users from gait \cite{Mantyjarvi2005,Derawi2010,Kwapisz2010}; subsequent work broadened the range of sensors, models, and deployment settings \cite{SpragerReview2015,Abuhamad2020}.

The difficult question is not whether two windows cut from one walk can be distinguished. It is whether a verifier enrolled from a person's ordinary walk still recognizes that person after exertion or on a later visit. Inertial signals change with task, pace, placement, and the body itself. Population-scale and in-the-wild studies have made clear that sample acquisition is part of the problem, not merely a preprocessing detail \cite{Ngo2014,Zou2020}. Clinical gait research also shows that attention and physical fatigue can change walking characteristics, although those studies do not by themselves establish the effect on biometric error \cite{Yogev2008,Granacher2010}.

We use DUO-GAIT, which recorded walking before and after fatigue in a single-task (ST) visit and a dual-task (DT) visit separated by approximately seven days \cite{Zhou2023}. Here, \emph{ST-control} means single-task walking before fatigue, \emph{ST-fatigue} means single-task walking after fatigue, \emph{DT-control} means dual-task walking before fatigue, and \emph{DT-fatigue} means dual-task walking after fatigue. We first quantify what goes wrong when a conventional user-specific SVM is enrolled from ST-control alone. We then test a different design: learn a reusable representation from the first-visit walks of other people, but ask a newly enrolled user to provide only ST-control walking. A new user is never asked to walk while fatigued or to perform the cognitive task for enrollment.

Three boundaries guide the interpretation. First, DT is also a later visit, so its difference from ST combines cognitive load, elapsed time, and sensor reattachment. Second, the representation learner sees \emph{other people's} ST-fatigue examples during population development, while a newly enrolled user still provides only ordinary ST-control walking. Third, the 11-second fusion result is an experimentally selected operating configuration: several durations were compared, and the selected duration is reported together with the complete sweep.

The paper makes three contributions: (i) a chronological, leakage-aware sacrum-IMU SVM assessment across all four conditions; (ii) a normal-only enrollment method for unseen users, based on a two-magnitude Siamese encoder trained with the first-visit recordings of development participants; and (iii) an analysis of false rejection, false acceptance, equal-error rate, participant variation, and the latency introduced by causal score fusion. Together, the two experiments move from identifying the cross-condition failure to evaluating a concrete representation-and-decision strategy for reducing it.

\subsection{Research questions}
The study is organized around three questions. \emph{RQ1} asks how much verification performance changes when a system enrolled on ST-control is evaluated after same-visit fatigue and during a later dual-task visit. This is answered by the statistical SVM, whose model and threshold are fixed before the three shifted conditions are scored. \emph{RQ2} asks whether population knowledge of fatigue can be learned once and transferred to a person who supplies only normal walking at enrollment. This is answered by the participant-disjoint Siamese experiment. \emph{RQ3} asks whether several consecutive comparisons provide a more reliable decision than one 2-second window. The fusion sweep answers this question while also exposing the corresponding delay.

These questions impose practical design requirements. Enrollment must remain normal-only for the new user; the later DT recordings must remain outside representation learning and threshold calibration; genuine and impostor trials must be evaluated together; and the decision must be causal. The resulting method is intended for continuous verification while the user is already walking, rather than instant device unlock. This distinction matters because an 11-second observation can be reasonable for background monitoring even though it would be too slow for an explicit login.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Inertial gait authentication}
Portable-device gait recognition predates current deep models. M\"antyj\"arvi \emph{et al.} used accelerometer-derived gait patterns for user identification \cite{Mantyjarvi2005}. Derawi \emph{et al.} studied unobtrusive mobile-phone verification and gait-cycle detection \cite{Derawi2010,DerawiCycle2010}, while Kwapisz \emph{et al.} explored cell-phone behavioral identification \cite{Kwapisz2010}. These papers motivate an authentication setting in which the user need not intentionally perform a biometric gesture, but they also leave acquisition context as a practical concern. Ngo \emph{et al.} examined a much larger inertial gait database \cite{Ngo2014}, and Sprager and Juri\v{c} review variation in sensors, features, and evaluation protocols \cite{SpragerReview2015}.

Feature-engineered systems remain useful as interpretable references. Higher-order statistics and context-aware motion features have both been applied to accelerometer authentication \cite{SpragerHOS2015,Primo2014,Nair2023}. Our SVM is a transparent within-user baseline with a fixed operating point and cross-condition probes. SVMs are a reasonable reference because they can fit nonlinear feature boundaries from limited enrollment data \cite{Cortes1995}, while the contrast between within-condition and later-condition performance reveals how strongly the operating point depends on acquisition context.

Deep gait studies have used convolutional features, recurrent sequences, multiple sensors, and wrist- or phone-based acquisition \cite{Dehzangi2017,Gadaleta2018,FernandezLopez2019,Tran2021,Cola2021,Zou2020}. They establish that learned representations can capture identity-bearing temporal structure, but their reported accuracies depend on device position, participant population, impostor construction, enrollment regime, and decision duration. For this reason, the present study reports complete verification error rates under one explicit protocol rather than comparing headline accuracy values across datasets \cite{Jain2004}.

\subsection{Variation across time, action, and condition}
Temporal adaptation has been studied over recordings separated by months, showing the value of explicitly learning features that remain useful as gait changes over time \cite{Permatasari2023}. Action-robust metric learning likewise suggests that same-person observations from different activities can be pulled together while different identities are separated \cite{Rasnayaka2022}. These findings motivate our population-learning strategy, but our protocol is deliberately different: representation learning is restricted to ST-control and ST-fatigue from the first visit, while the later dual-task visit remains the test. Multimodal and continuous-verification studies offer complementary routes to robustness \cite{Papavasileiou2017,Sim2007}; here a single sacrum sensor is retained so that the effect of condition change is not hidden by sensor fusion.

The timing of the walking task matters biologically as well as computationally. Executive attention contributes to gait control \cite{Yogev2008}, and an experimental study found that muscle fatigue altered gait characteristics under single- and dual-task conditions, with effects varying by age and measure \cite{Granacher2010}. We therefore do not impose a monotonic rule that DT-fatigue must always be harder than ST-fatigue for every participant. The recordings sample different walks on different days; individual compensatory strategies and attachment changes may alter the score in either direction. The DUO-GAIT dataset was designed to expose these conditions \cite{Zhou2023}, but it does not turn the later DT visit into a controlled isolation of cognitive load alone.

\subsection{Metric learning and score aggregation}
Our encoder maps short IMU windows into a common space, following the general idea of learned verification embeddings \cite{Schroff2015}. Batch-hard triplet learning chooses difficult same-person and different-person examples within a minibatch \cite{Hermans2017}. A convolutional front end represents local motion patterns; a bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) layer summarizes their sequence \cite{Hochreiter1997,Schuster1997}. These ingredients are established, not claimed as new in isolation. The question here is whether their combination, under normal-only enrollment and a held-out later visit, yields useful false-rejection and false-acceptance behavior. Averaging consecutive comparison scores is a separate decision-time intervention, conceptually related to repeated evidence in continuous verification \cite{Sim2007}; it changes latency and score calibration and must be evaluated as such.

\section{Data and Evaluation Design}
\subsection{DUO-GAIT and sacrum recordings}
DUO-GAIT contains recordings from 16 participants wearing inertial measurement units at several body locations, including the sacrum \cite{Zhou2023}. We use the sacrum triaxial accelerometer and triaxial gyroscope sampled at 128 Hz. One sensor position keeps the acquisition story simple and avoids treating additional body placements as independent observations. For each participant, we analyze ST-control and ST-fatigue from the single-task visit, and DT-control and DT-fatigue from the later dual-task visit. The dataset's dual task involved concurrent mental arithmetic, and fatigue followed an exercise protocol described by its creators \cite{Zhou2023}. These are laboratory conditions, not samples of every kind of real-world fatigue or divided attention.

An unusual ST-control sacrum recording for participant 07 extends well beyond the concurrent sensor recordings. We retain this participant by restricting that particular sacrum file to the timestamp interval 769.53125--1146.875~s, matching the corresponding ST-control sensor interval. The other three recordings for participant 07 are not trimmed in this way. This is a recording-alignment correction, not deletion of an inconvenient participant; the final cohort remains 16. The trimming rule was chosen after observing the recording discrepancy and should be disclosed when reproducing the experiment.

Both pipelines use 256-sample windows (2~s) with a 128-sample hop (50\% overlap). Adjacent windows consequently share half their raw samples. We retain chronological order and separate the relevant partitions by raw-sample boundaries where a split occurs. A random row-wise train/test split would place nearly identical portions of a walk on both sides of the split and could give an optimistic estimate. This does not imply that overlap itself is forbidden: overlap improves temporal coverage, provided shared raw samples do not cross enrollment, validation, and test boundaries. Fig.~\ref{fig:timeline} sets out the four recordings on a common timeline together with the partitions each experiment draws from them.

\subsection{Threat model and deployment assumptions}
The task is verification, not closed-set identification. The claimant states an identity, and the system decides whether the observed walk is sufficiently consistent with that identity's enrollment template. Genuine trials use the claimed participant's own probes. Zero-effort impostor trials use another participant's natural walk against the claimed template; the impostor does not imitate the target. This is the standard first security test for a behavioral verifier, although it does not cover replay, sensor substitution, deliberate mimicry, or adversarial manipulation.

The intended deployment is a wearable or body-mounted device that already has access to a sacrum-like IMU stream. The system operates only when walking is detected; absence of walking is an availability condition rather than evidence that the user is an impostor. Enrollment occurs during an ordinary walk and creates a compact user template. Later comparisons run in the background and may be combined with a conventional unlock method when the score is uncertain. The method therefore reduces the burden of condition-specific enrollment: fatigue examples from the new user, a second visit, and a concurrent cognitive task are not required.

Table~\ref{tab:protocols} separates the two experiments. The first is a diagnosis using participant-specific classifiers. The second evaluates a reusable population encoder on identities excluded from its development. Keeping these protocols explicit prevents three common confusions: model-development participants are not the newly enrolled users; a validation fold is not the final evaluation fold; and the presence of population fatigue data does not mean that the evaluation user supplied fatigued enrollment data.

\subsection{Claim-level verification and error rates}
At verification time a person claims an enrolled identity $u$. A probe from $u$ is a \emph{genuine} attempt; a probe from any other study participant against $u$'s template is an \emph{impostor} attempt. If $A(s,t)$ indicates that score $s$ is accepted at threshold $t$, false-rejection and false-acceptance rates for identity $u$ and condition $c$ are
\begin{align}
\mathrm{FRR}_{u,c}&=\frac{\#\{\text{rejected genuine attempts}\}}
{\#\{\text{genuine attempts}\}},\\
\mathrm{FAR}_{u,c}&=\frac{\#\{\text{accepted impostor attempts}\}}
{\#\{\text{impostor attempts}\}}.
\end{align}
We report a participant macro-average, $16^{-1}\sum_u r_{u,c}$, so a participant with a longer recording does not dominate a condition's summary. Parenthesized standard deviations describe variation \emph{between participant rates}, not confidence intervals and not variation among overlapping windows. The system's observed FAR at evaluation may differ from its 1\% \emph{development target}; that target is not a guarantee about later visits or unseen identities. Equal-error rate (EER) is reported only where an evaluation ROC is computed. Because EER selects a threshold using evaluation labels, it describes score separability retrospectively, not the error of the fixed-threshold authenticator \cite{Jain2004}.

FRR and FAR answer different operational questions. FRR measures inconvenience and loss of access for the legitimate user, whereas FAR measures acceptance of an impostor under the tested threat model. EER is the point on a detection-error curve where the two rates are equal; it summarizes how well genuine and impostor scores separate when the threshold is allowed to move. Standard deviation is not an alternative performance metric. It shows whether the participant-specific error rates are consistent or dominated by a few difficult users. Accordingly, the main conclusions use fixed-threshold FRR/FAR, supported by EER, detection-error curves, and participant-level dispersion.

\section{Experiment I: Statistical-Feature SVM}
\subsection{Features and user-specific models}
The problem assessment begins with a conventional, explainable verifier. For each window we form the six axis signals and the two vector magnitudes
\begin{align}
m_a(t)&=\sqrt{a_x(t)^2+a_y(t)^2+a_z(t)^2},\\
m_g(t)&=\sqrt{g_x(t)^2+g_y(t)^2+g_z(t)^2}.
\end{align}
For each of these eight signals, the implementation extracts 11 time-domain summaries: mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum, range, interquartile range, median absolute deviation, root-mean-square value, skewness, and kurtosis. The resulting vector has 88 components. These statistics are not claimed to be a canonical set from one paper; they cover level, spread, shape, and energy while retaining an inspectable baseline, in the spirit of earlier feature-based inertial systems \cite{SpragerHOS2015,Nair2023}.

One radial-basis-function SVM is trained per enrolled participant against windows of the remaining participants. Standardization is fitted on training data only. A grid over $C\in\{0.1,1,10,100\}$ and $\gamma\in\{\mathrm{scale},0.001,0.01,0.1\}$ is selected by five-fold \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} on training blocks, using ROC area as the selection measure. Grouping avoids splitting a short chronological block's overlapping windows across internal folds. The implementation uses scikit-learn \cite{Pedregosa2011}; the SVM formulation follows \cite{Cortes1995}. Cross-validation is for model selection within the training portion, not a replacement for a held-out test.

\subsection{Chronological calibration}
For each ST-control recording, windows are ordered in time and divided approximately 60/20/20 into training, threshold-development, and baseline-test portions, with guard windows at boundaries so the adjacent partitions share no raw samples. The participant's ST-control training windows are the positive class. Other participants' eligible ST-control windows serve as negatives. A separate threshold for each enrolled participant is chosen on ST-control validation scores to satisfy an empirical impostor acceptance target of at most 1\%. The same threshold is then applied unchanged to that participant's baseline ST-control test windows and to all three other conditions. No ST-fatigue or DT recording is used to tune this baseline threshold.

The SVM therefore answers a narrow but important question: \emph{How far does a verifier trained on an ordinary walk drift when the same identity is probed in another condition?} The answer is not a direct estimate of what the later population-trained model will achieve, because the two experiments differ in training identities, fatigue exposure, score type, threshold structure, and decision duration. Their comparison is a design reference, not a matched controlled ablation.

\subsection{What the assessment shows}
Table~\ref{tab:svm} shows a low held-out baseline error but sharply larger errors outside the enrollment condition. The ST-fatigue comparison stays within the ST visit yet raises mean FRR to 19.13\% and mean FAR to 11.26\%. DT-control raises mean FRR to 23.04\% while also increasing FAR relative to baseline. DT-fatigue is most difficult in aggregate, with 54.33\% mean FRR. In biometric terms, both convenience and security change: a rule that rejects more legitimate users can still accept more impostors from the shifted condition. \section{Experiment II: Normal-Only Enrollment with a Population Encoder}
\subsection{Design objective and allowed data}
The second experiment keeps the enrollment burden realistic. A new user supplies only ST-control walking, yet the reusable encoder may learn from ST-control and ST-fatigue of \emph{different} development users. The later DT-control and DT-fatigue recordings are held out as cross-visit tests. We split the 16 participants into four deterministic outer folds. In each fold, 12 participants contribute development data and four completely unseen participants are enrolled and evaluated. Every participant appears in the unseen group once. Within each development participant and each first-visit condition, chronological blocks are divided 80/20 for learning and validation, with windows crossing the boundary removed. The validation portion is used for early stopping and a fold-wide score threshold; no evaluation participant or DT condition enters training or calibration.

The word \emph{Siamese} describes shared weights, not a requirement for two separate network files or a binary pair label. The same encoder maps every window into an embedding. The model is optimized with a triplet objective rather than explicit $(\text{pair},0/1)$ training. For each ST-control anchor $a$, a same-person window $p$ may come from ST-control or ST-fatigue, and a different-person window $n$ may come from either condition. Within a minibatch we take the farthest available positive and closest negative and minimize
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{triplet}}=\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_{a\in A}\max\bigl(0,\|z_a-z_p\|_2-\|z_a-z_n\|_2+0.2\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $A$ contains only ST-control anchors and $z$ is a unit-length embedding. This arrangement explicitly asks the representation to keep a development user's normal and fatigued windows close while separating other users. An auxiliary identity-classification head over the 12 development identities contributes weighted cross-entropy during training; it is discarded before enrolling the four new users. The goal is transfer of a representation, not identification of new users by a closed-set classifier.

\subsection{Two-magnitude input and network}
Rather than using eight axis-and-magnitude channels, the evaluated system computes just $m_a(t)$ and $m_g(t)$ from the raw six-axis sacrum windows. These are \emph{time series of magnitudes}, not two scalar summary features. Each input has shape $256\times2$. Taking vector norms removes changes caused solely by rotating the sensor coordinate axes, which is a plausible source of cross-visit variation. It does not remove changes in motion dynamics, nonrigid sensor displacement, or differences in how the unit is strapped on. The motivation is related to the general temporal-robustness problem studied by Permatasari \emph{et al.} \cite{Permatasari2023}, but no claim is made that their algorithm or exact input representation is reproduced here.

Channel normalization is estimated on development \emph{learning} windows and reused unchanged for validation and unseen-user evaluation. Three one-dimensional convolutional blocks (32, 64, and 96 channels) capture local temporal patterns. A 64-unit-per-direction BiLSTM processes the resulting sequence. Attention-weighted and maximum temporal pools are concatenated, projected through a 128-unit layer to a 64-dimensional vector, and $L_2$-normalized. The auxiliary classifier acts on the embedding only during learning. The default optimizer is AdamW with learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$; dropout is 0.25, triplet margin 0.2, and identity-loss weight 0.3. Batches draw eight participants and three windows per first-visit condition for each, with 100 batches per epoch. Training runs for at most 40 epochs with patience seven, selecting a development-validation checkpoint. These settings describe the implemented run, not a claim of exhaustive hyperparameter optimization; Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters} collects them in full.

The convolutional stages reduce the temporal length from 256 to 64 steps while expanding the channel representation from 2 to 96. If $h_t\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$ is the bidirectional recurrent output at step $t$, the attention layer calculates
\begin{equation}
e_t=w^{\mathsf T}h_t+b,\qquad
\alpha_t=\frac{\exp(e_t)}{\sum_j\exp(e_j)},\qquad
h_{\mathrm{att}}=\sum_t\alpha_t h_t.
\end{equation}
The attention summary and elementwise temporal maximum are concatenated. This gives the projection layer both a weighted description of the full walk and the strongest observed response in each recurrent channel. The final normalization,
\begin{equation}
z=\frac{q}{\max(\|q\|_2,\epsilon)},
\end{equation}
places all embeddings on the unit hypersphere. Distance is then driven by direction in the learned identity space rather than arbitrary vector scale.

The auxiliary classification term stabilizes identity learning across the development population. With participant label $y$ and classifier logits $r$, the complete training objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{triplet}}+0.3\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(r,y).
\end{equation}
Triplet loss supplies the verification geometry; cross-entropy encourages the embedding to preserve information that distinguishes the development identities. The classification head is absent at inference, so an unseen participant can be enrolled without assigning that person to one of the development classes.

\subsection{Enrollment, scoring, and operating point}
For each unseen user, the complete ST-control recording forms the only enrollment data. Because every probe comes from the later visit, no part of the first-visit normal walk needs to be withheld. The encoder maps them to 64-dimensional vectors. Their average is $L_2$-normalized to obtain one user template. A probe is compared with every claimed template; distance $d=\|z_{\mathrm{probe}}-z_{\mathrm{template}}\|_2$ is small for a likely match. On the unit sphere, Euclidean distance is monotonic in cosine similarity, so this is a consistent comparison score. Impostor attempts are generated by presenting each other evaluation participant's DT probes against the claimed participant's template. This is a closed-cohort impostor test; it does not include skilled mimicry or an external attack population.

More formally, if $E_u$ contains the enrollment embeddings for user $u$, the stored template is
\begin{equation}
T_u=\frac{\frac{1}{|E_u|}\sum_{z\in E_u}z}{\left\|\frac{1}{|E_u|}\sum_{z\in E_u}z\right\|_2}.
\end{equation}
For a sequence of probe windows ending at index $i$, the fused comparison score is
\begin{align}
\bar d_{u,i}^{(K)}&=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=i-K+1}^{i}\|z_j-T_u\|_2,\\
\operatorname{accept}(u,i)&=\mathbb{1}\!\left[\bar d_{u,i}^{(K)}\le\tau\right].
\end{align}
The selected system uses $K=10$. Template construction is an average over enrollment embeddings, while score fusion is an average over comparison distances; they serve different purposes. The first summarizes the enrolled identity once. The second reduces short-duration fluctuation in a stream of later authentication evidence.

For each fold, one numerical threshold is chosen from held-out \emph{development} ST-control and ST-fatigue scores at an empirical 1\% FAR target. The same fold threshold applies to all four unseen users and both DT conditions; there is no user-specific or condition-specific threshold. Different outer folds necessarily learn different encoders and may yield different numerical thresholds. That is four repetitions of one protocol, not four operational settings chosen after observing a test condition.

The evaluated variant averages the current and previous nine window-level distances for the same claimed user and probe sequence, then applies the development threshold. Ten 2-second windows at a 1-second hop cover 11 seconds at the first full decision; after that, a new rolling decision can be made each second. Averaging is causal because no future window contributes to an earlier decision. The complete duration sweep is reported so that the selected 11-second configuration and its latency trade-off remain visible.

\subsection{End-to-end training and verification flow}
Figure~\ref{fig:system} summarizes the full method. During population development, every raw sacrum window first passes through the same magnitude conversion and normalization. A minibatch contains several participants so that each anchor has both same-person and different-person candidates. ST-control supplies the anchors because it matches the intended new-user enrollment condition. ST-control and ST-fatigue supply the positive pool for that participant, forcing the encoder to represent identity features shared across pre- and post-fatigue walking. Windows from the remaining minibatch participants form the negative pool. Batch-hard selection then identifies the farthest same-person example and nearest different-person example available for each anchor. These difficult comparisons generate the strongest useful gradient while easier comparisons that already satisfy the margin contribute zero triplet loss.

After each epoch, the encoder is evaluated on chronological validation windows that were not used for gradient updates. Early stopping retains the checkpoint with the best validation objective rather than the final epoch automatically. The selected checkpoint is then frozen: convolutional, recurrent, attention, and projection weights no longer change during enrollment or test. This separation is essential because the later four evaluation participants represent people joining the system after population development.

Enrollment of an unseen user is deliberately short conceptually even though it includes many overlapping windows. The system receives the user's ST-control recording, converts it into magnitude sequences, embeds every sequence with the frozen encoder, and averages those embeddings to form $T_u$. Only this 64-value normalized vector is needed for subsequent comparisons. The original enrollment windows need not be repeatedly processed during authentication. Each later probe follows the identical magnitude, normalization, and encoder path. Its distance to $T_u$ becomes the window-level comparison score.

At decision time, the verifier keeps a first-in, first-out buffer containing the most recent ten distances for the claim. The buffer is claimant-specific; scores from different users or conditions are never mixed. Once ten distances are available, their arithmetic mean is compared with the fold threshold. A mean at or below $\tau$ is accepted because smaller Euclidean distance indicates greater similarity. A mean above $\tau$ is rejected. The buffer then advances by one window, enabling a new causal decision every second. This flow uses one model architecture, one scoring rule, and one threshold rule across both DT-control and DT-fatigue; the system does not first classify the condition and does not select a condition-specific verifier.

\subsection{Design rationale}
The method combines three choices that address different parts of the condition-shift problem. Magnitude conversion addresses coordinate-frame variation. The sacrum sensor records motion along device axes, so a changed attachment angle can redistribute the same physical movement across $x$, $y$, and $z$. The vector norm retains overall acceleration and angular-velocity intensity while removing that redistribution under an ideal rigid rotation. It is therefore applied before the learned encoder, rather than expecting the small development cohort to learn every possible orientation from examples.

Metric learning addresses the difference between population training and enrollment of a new identity. A conventional 16-class network would learn to choose among the development participants and would have no output class for a later user. In contrast, the encoder learns a distance space. Once the weights are frozen, any person can be represented by averaging normal-walk embeddings; adding a new user does not require retraining the network. The ST-control anchor restriction makes the reference point consistent with enrollment, while the mixed-condition positive pool teaches that fatigue-related movement from the same development person should remain closer than another person's movement. Negatives drawn from both first-visit conditions also prevent the model from using condition alone as a shortcut for identity.

Temporal fusion addresses decision variance. A 2-second window may cover a slightly unusual step, a turn transition, a brief change in speed, or sensor noise. Consecutive distances contain repeated evidence about the same claim, and their mean is less sensitive to one extreme value. The 50\% overlap gives a new window every second, so the method gains a longer effective observation without waiting for ten disjoint 2-second segments. The cost is correlation and initial latency, both of which are made explicit in the protocol and duration sweep.

Several alternatives were considered conceptually but are outside the selected system. Condition-specific user templates would require either condition labels at runtime or condition-specific enrollment from the user. Test-time template updating could follow gradual behavioral drift, but a false acceptance could also contaminate the stored template. A global classifier over the 16 recorded participants would simplify training but would not support unseen enrollment. The chosen architecture instead keeps one shared encoder, one normal-only user template, one distance function, and one threshold rule. This makes the deployment path straightforward and keeps the experiment focused on whether population condition knowledge transfers to an unseen person.

\subsection{How the two experiments relate}
The SVM diagnoses the original problem across all four conditions. The population model then asks whether seeing \emph{other users'} same-visit fatigue while learning a representation helps normal-enrolled unseen users on a later dual-task visit. Numerically, the fused model's mean FRR is below the SVM's on DT-control (16.33 versus 23.04\%) and DT-fatigue (47.55 versus 54.33\%). Its mean FAR is above the SVM's on DT-control (11.41 versus 10.15\%) and below it on DT-fatigue (13.06 versus 13.95\%). The SVM uses participant-specific models, whereas the population encoder transfers a common representation to unseen identities and aggregates 11 seconds of evidence. Together, they demonstrate the initial condition mismatch and the performance obtained by the proposed normal-enrollment strategy.

Under normal-only enrollment, the proposed model reduces DT-control FRR by 6.71 percentage points relative to the statistical assessment and reduces DT-fatigue FRR by 6.78 points. DT-fatigue FAR also falls by 0.89 points, while DT-control FAR rises by 1.26 points. These differences are contextual rather than a controlled ablation. The two experiments differ in training identities, threshold structure, impostor set, and decision duration, so the subtraction above cannot be attributed to the representation alone; establishing that would require a matched protocol with the same enrollment, calibration, and decision length for both systems. The strongest balanced result is obtained on DT-control, where the 11-second model reaches 16.33\% FRR and 11.41\% FAR. The remaining DT-fatigue overlap identifies the next engineering target: preserving the orientation tolerance of magnitude input while recovering identity-bearing directional or multi-sensor information.

\subsection{Participant heterogeneity and error concentration}
The macro-average gives every participant equal influence, but it does not imply that all participants experience the mean error. The large FRR standard deviations show a mixture of relatively stable users and users whose later-visit genuine scores move beyond the development threshold. This concentration is evident in the participant-level standard deviations of Tables~\ref{tab:svm} and~\ref{tab:proposed}, which are of the same magnitude as the means themselves. Such behavior is expected in a behavioral biometric: the observable trait and its stability depend on both the person and the recording context.

This pattern also explains why network depth alone does not determine cross-visit success. A high-capacity model may lower training loss while learning boundaries that transfer poorly to a particular later walk. Participant-disjoint folds directly test that transfer. Magnitude input reduces coordinate-orientation dependence, and triplet learning introduces fatigue variation from development users, but an unseen participant may express a gait change outside the range represented by those users. A larger and more varied development population would expose the encoder to more plausible within-person changes and more difficult between-person similarities.

For operational use, heterogeneity suggests a quality-aware response rather than an immediate irreversible rejection. Low-motion, transitional, or irregular windows can be deferred; stable walking can accumulate evidence; and a persistently adverse score can trigger another authentication factor. This policy does not change the biometric rates reported here, but it translates a continuous score stream into a safer application workflow. The fusion stage follows that principle by basing a decision on sustained evidence instead of one isolated 2-second segment.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
\subsection{What can and cannot be attributed to the conditions}
ST-control versus ST-fatigue is a same-visit comparison in the SVM assessment and directly exposes a post-fatigue recording contrast. DT-control and DT-fatigue are from another visit. Even if DT-control has lower FRR than ST-fatigue for a particular person, that does not invalidate the dataset or require that one recording be mislabeled: the walks are not a controlled trajectory along a single scalar ``difficulty'' axis. The later visit includes cognitive-task instructions, elapsed time, possible sensor reattachment, and natural day-to-day variation. Fatigue may also be expressed differently across the two visits. The protocol can establish which \emph{recorded condition} is harder for this verifier; it cannot decompose an isolated cognitive, time, and fatigue effect from these data alone. A future factorial design with matched same-day ST/DT recordings and multiple repeat visits would be needed for that causal claim.

The magnitude choice deserves the same care. A vector norm is invariant to an ideal rigid rotation of the coordinate frame. It is not invariant to a changed walking style, a shifted sacrum attachment, or alterations in acceleration magnitude. Removing axis direction therefore trades one source of cross-visit variation for a possible loss of identity-bearing information, and the present results cannot separate the two effects. Settling this requires a matched ablation in which the same protocol is run with six-axis and magnitude inputs, together with controlled rotation augmentation, rather than inference from the reported rates alone.


\subsection{Calibration and security}
A development FAR target of 1\% was not maintained on cross-visit evaluation. This is a central result, not a formatting issue. The threshold was calibrated from first-visit, unfused scores, whereas the fused test scores come from later-visit walks. A distribution shift can change genuine and impostor scores in different ways. Averaging reduces short-term noise but does not guarantee that the development threshold remains safe. An attacker test here consists only of other participants' natural walking; there is no imitation, replay, carried-device substitution, or adversarial perturbation. The reported FAR should therefore not be interpreted as resistance to active attacks. A secure system should assess presentation attacks and define conservative fallback behavior \cite{Jain2004,Abuhamad2020}.

The use of overlapping windows also affects statistical interpretation. Guarding split boundaries protects against literal raw-sample overlap between learning and validation, but successive evaluation decisions remain correlated. The between-participant SD correctly shows heterogeneity but is not a precision estimate for a much larger population. Confidence intervals generated by resampling individual windows would be misleading. A future analysis should bootstrap at participant and recording level, keep each complete visit together, and publish detection-error trade-off curves and score files at predeclared operating points.

\subsection{Sample size, selection, and generalizability}
The 16-participant laboratory cohort supports controlled participant-disjoint experiments, but a larger population is needed to estimate field reliability and represent a wider range of gait change. Each outer fold tests four people, and the zero-effort impostor population comes from the same dataset. The sub-07 trim, selected input channels, architecture revisions, and 11-second fusion duration were documented during iterative investigation. A larger follow-up should freeze the complete preprocessing, encoder, fusion, and threshold rule before evaluation. Repeated visits, varied walking speeds, deliberate sensor reattachments, and additional sites would test how broadly the observed gains transfer.

The SVM and encoder have intentionally different roles: Table~\ref{tab:svm} establishes the problem with a conventional verifier, and Table~\ref{tab:proposed} evaluates the proposed population representation. A future matched ablation can additionally compute SVM scores over the same 11-second decision units and hold the same impostor attempts fixed. That experiment would isolate the contributions of input representation, metric learning, and temporal aggregation more precisely.

\section{Reproducibility}

The source data are publicly available through DUO-GAIT~\cite{Zhou2023}. The implementation distinguishes the processed raw windows used by the proposed model from the statistical-feature tables used by the SVM baseline. The proposed model reads six-axis inertial windows and derives acceleration and angular-velocity magnitude signals internally; it does not train on the 88-dimensional statistical-feature matrix.

For each window, the processing pipeline records the participant identity, experimental condition, starting sample, and temporal block. The experimental outputs include participant-level fold assignments, normalization parameters, model checkpoints, operating thresholds, and genuine and impostor comparison scores. The statistical baseline separately stores its participant-level results and summary statistics. Releasing the source code, de-identified comparison scores, protocol manifest, configuration parameters, and exact execution commands would allow the reported threshold behavior and performance measures to be independently examined. Dataset licensing and participant-consent requirements should be reviewed before derived artifacts are released.


\section{Limitations}

This study has several limitations. First, the experiments contain only 16 participants. Participant-disjoint cross-validation allows every participant to be evaluated as an unseen user, but the small cohort limits the extent to which the findings can be generalized to a broader population. The large standard deviations also indicate substantial differences in performance between participants. Evaluation on a larger and more diverse population is therefore necessary.

Second, the ST and DT recordings were collected during visits separated by seven days. The DT conditions consequently combine the effects of cognitive load, elapsed time, and possible sensor reattachment differences. These effects cannot be separated using the present data. The results should therefore be interpreted as performance under a combined dual-task and cross-session change rather than as an isolated measurement of cognitive load.

Third, the study uses only the sacrum sensor. Although this location captures motion close to the body’s center of mass, performance may differ for smartphones, smartwatches, and sensors attached at less controlled positions. The experiment also assumes that walking has already been detected and that the sensor remains consistently positioned. These assumptions may not hold under free-living conditions.

Finally, the system is evaluated using recordings collected in structured walking experiments. The results do not yet demonstrate robustness during daily activities involving changes in walking surface, footwear, speed, carrying load, injury, device orientation, or longer time intervals.


\section{Future Work}

Future work will evaluate the proposed method on a substantially larger and more diverse participant population. New data collection should include repeated recordings over several weeks or months and should vary fatigue, cognitive load, and sensor reattachment independently. This would make it possible to distinguish the contribution of each factor from ordinary cross-session gait variation.

Further work should investigate representations that learn condition-related variation from development participants without requiring a newly enrolled user to provide fatigued or dual-task walking samples. Secure template adaptation is another promising direction because it could allow the enrolled template to follow gradual behavioral changes. However, any update mechanism must prevent rejected or uncertain samples from contaminating the legitimate user’s template.

The method should also be evaluated using realistic mobile and wearable sensor placements and under free-living conditions. Future comparisons between the statistical baseline and the proposed model should use identical test participants, probe windows, impostor attempts, decision durations, and threshold-selection procedures. Finally, more consistent performance across participants should be prioritized, since reducing the mean error alone would not address users for whom the system continues to produce extremely high false rejection rates.

\section{Conclusion}
Gait collected from normal walking can look reliable until the same identity is probed under a different condition. In DUO-GAIT sacrum data, a chronological, user-specific statistical SVM has 1.49\% mean baseline FRR but substantially larger error on same-visit fatigue and later dual-task walks; FAR rises as well. The proposed population-trained encoder transfers first-visit condition knowledge to unseen users who provide only normal walking at enrollment. With two magnitude time series and an 11-second causal decision, it obtains 16.33/11.41\% FRR/FAR on DT-control and 47.55/13.06\% on DT-fatigue. Relative to the statistical assessment, false rejection is reduced under both later-visit conditions while DT-fatigue FAR is also reduced. The results support population-level condition learning as a practical direction for normal-only gait enrollment and show where additional population diversity and condition modeling can produce the next gains.
